# Episode: The Mirror That Was Perfectly Wrong

Nineteen ninety. NASA launches its flagship space telescope, and the first pictures come back blurry.
Not a software bug. Not a dirty lens. The mirror itself was wrong.
Meet Hubble. A bus-sized observatory circling about five hundred and forty kilometres above Earth.
At its heart sits a two point four metre mirror, built to focus starlight into one sharp point.
NASA asked for a surface smooth to about ten nanometres. Engineers spent years grinding and polishing it.
They nailed it. The mirror was flawlessly smooth. Just the wrong shape.
Near the edge, it was ground two point two micrometres too flat. A human hair is fifty times thicker.
Here is why that matters. A good mirror sends every ray, centre or edge, to the very same focus.
Hubble's mirror sends the edge rays to a different spot. That is called spherical aberration.
So every star becomes a bright point wrapped in a fuzzy halo, and most of the light lands where it should not.
How did nobody catch it? The mirror was checked with a test device called a null corrector.
The null corrector was assembled wrong. One lens sat one point three millimetres out of place.
A measuring rod had a worn spot, the laser bounced off the wrong surface, and the instrument was built to match the mistake.
The test reported a perfect mirror. It was. For the wrong shape.
Two other tests did show the flaw. They were dismissed as less accurate.
Swapping a mirror in orbit was impossible. So engineers built glasses for a telescope.
If you know the exact error, you can cancel it. Add mirrors with the opposite error, and the light comes out right.
The fix had two parts. A new camera with correction built in, and Costar, a box of small mirrors for the other instruments.
December nineteen ninety-three. Space Shuttle Endeavour reaches out and catches Hubble.
Five spacewalks in eleven days. Astronauts swapped the hardware by hand, in orbit.
January nineteen ninety-four. The new view of galaxy M one hundred is razor sharp.
Hubble went on to spend three decades rewriting astronomy.
The lesson. When three tests disagree, do not trust the one you like. Find out why.
A perfect result from a broken test is just a confident mistake.
